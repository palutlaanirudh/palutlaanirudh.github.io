Current Project

Main project · Janelia · in progress

I am building an ML-guided directed evolution system for designing better biosensors, and using it to work on GCaMP, the green calcium indicator used to watch neurons fire. A model predicts the fitness of variants we've never measured, and an in-silico evolution loop proposes which ones to actually build and test. GCaMP is our first target, but the goal is an approach that generalizes across sensors. I drive this work as part of a joint project between the labs of Dr. Srini Turaga (computational/ML) and Dr. Alison Tebo (protein engineering).

Generalization of Protein Foundation Models for Engineered Fluorescent Biosensors

How well do protein foundation models (PFMs) actually predict biosensor function, especially in the out-of-distribution regimes that matter for engineering, like unseen sequence regions or variants with higher fitness than the training data? We built train-test splits that isolate each of these regimes and found that generalizing to them is a fundamentally different problem from what random splits measure; standard random-split benchmarks can substantially overstate how useful a PFM will be for real design. This work appeared as workshop papers at GenBio and FM4LS @ ICML 2026.

Nature Methods Co-author 2025

Glutamate indicators with increased sensitivity and tailored deactivation rates

iGluSnFR4 is the latest generation of genetically encoded glutamate sensors for imaging synaptic transmission in vivo. My contribution to this project was modeling epistatic interactions between mutation sites with a pairwise generalized linear model. We found that interaction strength fell off with distance, and many of the strongest interactions were between different protein domains.

Cognitive Neurodynamics First author 2023

Criticality in Alzheimer’s and healthy brains: insights from phase-ordering

My Master's work, where I applied the statistical mechanics of phase transitions to the brain. The brain is thought to sit near a critical point like a material poised between two phases. We studied this behavior using a spin-lattice model from resting-state fMRI to ask how that proximity differs between Alzheimer's-afflicted and cognitively normal brains, and which class of criticality is actually at play. In both groups the temporal correlations followed a stretched exponential, pointing to the spin-glass class rather than the simpler Ising class, and our findings suggest that Alzheimer's brains are further from criticality than healthy ones.

03

Background

I got my Bachelor's in Computer Science and my Master's in Computational Natural Sciences from the International Institute of Information Technology, Hyderabad (IIIT-H). I had a strong interest in machine learning throughout my coursework, and I worked across a wide variety of domains in biology. During my final year, I collaborated with John Koberstein, Srini Turaga and Alison Tebo at Janelia to apply machine learning techniques to biosensor function prediction. This experience solidified my interest in the intersection of machine learning and biology. I find the challenge of applying computational methods to complex biological systems fascinating, and it was this interest that led me to join Janelia Research Campus.

I have spent the past two years at Janelia driving my own project, where I build ML models to predict biosensor function and use them to guide directed evolution. My computer science training gives me the tools I need to build these systems, and my computational sciences training taught me to think about the underlying biology. Janelia taught me to keep my models grounded in wet-lab evaluation and not just benchmarks. I learned not only to build models from noisy experimental data, but also to collect the right data to improve them.
